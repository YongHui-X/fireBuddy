## Core investing frame for Singapore users

Singapore investors usually need to separate money into three buckets:

1. Near-term cash: emergency fund, upcoming housing payments, insurance premiums,
   taxes, and planned large purchases. This should not be exposed to equity risk.
2. Medium-term low-risk assets: Singapore Savings Bonds, T-bills, fixed deposits,
   money market funds, or cash management products. Match this bucket to the
   timing of known expenses.
3. Long-term growth assets: diversified equities, broad-market ETFs, selected
   unit trusts, or a professionally managed portfolio. This bucket is for goals
   measured in years or decades, not months.

The first question is not "which product is best". It is:
- When do I need the money?
- Can I tolerate a temporary drawdown?
- What happens if I lose my job or need cash during a market downturn?
- Is the money cash, CPF, or SRS money?

## Singapore-specific account types

Cash:
- Most flexible.
- Can be used for local and global investments.
- No CPF lock-up rules.
- Must still account for broker fees, foreign exchange spread, withholding tax,
  estate tax risk for some foreign assets, and product complexity.

CPF:
- CPF OA earns a government-set floor interest rate and is often also needed for
  housing. Investing CPF OA money introduces market risk and opportunity cost.
- CPFIS lets eligible members invest part of OA and SA savings in approved
  products. CPF Board states that new CPFIS investors must complete the
  Self-Awareness Questionnaire before investing.
- The first 20,000 dollars in OA and first 40,000 dollars in SA are protected
  from investment use. This matters because CPF savings have a risk-free base
  return and extra interest on the first combined balances.
- CPF investments should clear a higher bar than cash investments because the
  alternative is guaranteed CPF interest, especially for SA/RA-like retirement
  savings.

SRS:
- SRS is a voluntary tax-deferred retirement scheme.
- IRAS states the annual contribution cap is 15,300 dollars for Singapore
  Citizens and PRs, and 35,700 dollars for foreigners.
- Contributions reduce taxable income, but withdrawals are constrained by SRS
  rules. Early withdrawals can create tax and penalty consequences.
- SRS is usually most useful for people with meaningful taxable income and a
  long enough time horizon to invest the funds instead of leaving them idle.

## Product map

Singapore Savings Bonds:
- Issued by the Singapore Government through MAS.
- Designed for individual investors.
- MAS states that individuals can hold up to 200,000 dollars of Savings Bonds at
  any point.
- Useful for emergency reserves, medium-term cash, and defensive portfolio
  buffers because they can generally be redeemed with accrued interest.
- Not a replacement for long-term equity growth.

T-bills and SGS:
- Singapore Government Securities with different maturity and liquidity features.
- T-bills are short-duration instruments and do not pay coupons; they are issued
  at a discount and redeemed at face value.
- Unlike SSBs, T-bills are normally held to maturity. They are less flexible if
  the user may need the money suddenly.

REITs:
- MoneySense describes REITs as exchange-traded vehicles that allow investors to
  own property exposure without buying a whole property.
- Singapore REITs can provide income, but they still carry equity-like price
  volatility, interest-rate sensitivity, refinancing risk, tenant risk, and
  sector concentration risk.
- IRAS guidance indicates that some REIT distributions to individuals may be tax
  exempt, but tax treatment depends on the nature of the distribution and the
  investor's circumstances.

Broad-market ETFs and funds:
- Broad, diversified equity exposure is commonly used for long-term growth.
- For Singapore FIRE users, the main questions are domicile, tax treatment,
  dividend withholding, estate tax exposure, currency exposure, expense ratio,
  tracking error, liquidity, and whether the fund is available through cash,
  CPF, or SRS.
- Avoid presenting any ETF ticker as universally best. Ask about residency,
  investment horizon, account type, risk tolerance, and whether the user needs
  accumulating or distributing income.

Single stocks, sector bets, crypto, and leveraged products:
- These are concentration-risk or high-volatility products.
- They may fit a small satellite allocation for experienced users, but they
  should not be treated as the foundation of a basic FIRE plan.

## Portfolio construction principles

Use goal-based allocation:
- Emergency fund: cash or cash-like assets.
- Home down payment or renovation within 1 to 3 years: avoid equity risk.
- FIRE accumulation over 10 or more years: diversified growth assets can be
  appropriate if the user accepts volatility.
- FIRE drawdown within 0 to 5 years: build cash, SSB, T-bill, and bond buffers
  before depending on equity sales.

Diversify across:
- Asset class: cash, bonds, equities, property exposure.
- Geography: Singapore-only portfolios are concentrated in banks, REITs, and a
  small domestic market.
- Currency: spending is in SGD, but global equity exposure often introduces USD,
  EUR, GBP, or other currency exposure.
- Account type: cash, CPF, and SRS have different liquidity and tax treatment.

Rebalance deliberately:
- Rebalancing forces the investor to trim what has grown and add to what has
  lagged, according to the target allocation.
- Rebalancing frequency can be calendar-based or threshold-based.
- Do not rebalance so often that fees, taxes, or emotional trading dominate.

## Common Singapore mistakes to flag

- Investing emergency money in equities because the expected return is higher.
- Treating CPF OA as spare cash when it is also housing and retirement capital.
- Maximising SRS without investing it, resulting in cash drag.
- Chasing REIT yield without checking debt, lease expiry, sector exposure, and
  interest-rate sensitivity.
- Buying a foreign ETF or stock without understanding withholding tax and estate
  tax exposure.
- Ignoring currency mismatch between SGD spending and non-SGD assets.
- Assuming CPF LIFE alone will cover a desired retirement lifestyle.
- Using recent T-bill or SSB yields as if they are permanent.

## Answering style for the chatbot

When asked for investment help:
- Start by asking for goal, time horizon, account type, risk tolerance, and
  liquidity needs.
- Explain tradeoffs rather than prescribing a product.
- Separate official rules from common FIRE community practice.
- Mention that figures and tax rules should be verified against CPF, IRAS, MAS,
  and MoneySense before the user acts.
